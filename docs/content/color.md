# Color

Iowa's black and gold are among the most recognizable colors in higher education. Our color system builds on that foundation, defining roles for every color so they work together consistently across every format and medium.

## Brand

<hr>

### When to reach for this

Gold and black are Iowa's primary brand-recognition colors. White and neutral grays support the system by creating structure, contrast, readability, and space. Together, gold, black, white, and neutrals form the foundation of the Iowa visual identity and should dominate the overall composition before secondary or role-based colors are introduced.

### Guidance

- Black and gold should always be the most prominent colors used in any marketing materials.
- Use Iowa gold only at full strength. Black and gray may adjust into lighter or darker values, and white may shift only into near-white neutrals for surfaces. Altered gold loses brand recognition and can look off-brand.
- Don't use red or purple in brand and marketing color. They read as peer institutions.
- Don't pair gold text on white or white text on gold. Both fall below 4.5:1 and fail AA.
- Use white, not gold, for paragraph body text on black. Gold can work for emphasis or short display text, but white is more legible and accessible for sustained reading.
- Don't combine gold with accent colors.
- Don't use gold on gray backgrounds.

## Neutrals

<hr>

### When to reach for this

Neutral colors serve a supporting role in the system. They are used for backgrounds, borders, dividers, and reading surfaces where a full brand color would be too heavy. Use them to create structure and breathing room without competing with gold, black, or white.

### Use neutrals for

- Page and component backgrounds
- Reading surfaces
- Borders and dividers
- Table rows and interface structure
- Text hierarchy
- Light and dark mode foundations
- Presentation and slide backgrounds, gridlines, and chart structure

### Guidance

- Use neutrals to create structure and breathing room without competing with gold, black, or white.
- On white or light surfaces, darker neutrals carry text while lighter neutrals support backgrounds, rows, and dividers.
- On black or dark surfaces, the relationship reverses: light neutrals become foreground colors, while darker neutrals provide structure.
- Choose neutral steps by function first, not by reaching for "a gray." A neutral used for fine print, background, a divider, or a disabled control is doing a specific job. That role should guide the token choice, whether the work appears in a printed piece or an interface.

## Accents

<hr>

### When to reach for this

Reach for an accent when something needs to stand out from the rest of the layout, whether that's highlighting information, creating visual distinction, or differentiating data. Accents support the layout — they don't fill it. Use them to draw attention on small elements, not to cover large areas or carry the composition.

Accents work because they're used intentionally and sparingly. If you're reaching for one more than once or twice in a layout, that's usually a sign something else needs to change first, like hierarchy, spacing, or copy, not a sign you need another accent.

### Guidance

- Accent colors are not for large background fields. Use them only through approved styles for contained elements — Font Awesome icons, badges, tags, chart bars or data points, and similar structural moments — not for coloring a section, panel, or pages.
- Do not combine accent colors with gold.
- Keep accents to roughly 10–15% of a layout at most. Use primary colors and neutrals for the larger areas.
- Keep accents supporting structure, like section starts and stat emphasis. Limit accent colors as text. Headlines, labels, and body copy should stay in neutrals or primary colors. Save accent color for fills and small contained elements, unless there's a clear functional reason, like a link, to use it as text.
- Don't rely on accent blue alone to signal a link: underline links so they read without color.
- Don't allow an accent color to become the primary or signifying color for a department, unit, or campaign.
- Don't put white text on a light accent fill. Accents are for fills and icons, not text.

## Data visualization

<hr>

### When to use

Data colors are used for charts, graphs, and other visualizations where multiple categories need to be distinguished from each other. They are designed to be clear and accessible, but color should never be the only way data is differentiated. Always pair data colors with labels, patterns, or position.

### Use data visualization colors for

- Charts and graphs
- Chart Legends
- Charts that use color to distinguish multiple groups or categories

### Guidance

- Use the data scales in the order they appear, since the sequence is designed to provide the strongest contrast between adjacent colors and keep chart series as distinguishable as possible.
- Use the data palette for charts and other multi-series visualizations.
- Use the light data scale on light surfaces.
- Use the dark data scale on black or dark surfaces.
- Do not distinguish chart series by hue alone.
- Pair data colors with labels, patterns, position, or other non-color indicators.

## Semantic

<hr>

### When to use

Semantic colors use widely recognized color meanings to communicate status, risk, or required action. The design system does not invent these meanings; it applies familiar associations from safety, signage, wayfinding, status indicators, and everyday use.

Because semantic meanings depend on recognition, semantic colors must be used consistently and sparingly across digital, print, signage, and environmental contexts. Use them only when color is communicating meaning, not decoration.

### Use semantic colors for

- Alerts and messages
- Validation, confirmation, and error states
- System feedback and status indicators
- Safety, caution, stop, or danger cues
- Informational notices and wayfinding cues
- Environmental or operational contexts where color identifies a role, risk, or required action

### Guidance

- Do not use semantic colors decoratively, as an accent, or to draw attention to non-semantic content.
- Pair every semantic color with a non-color cue (icon, symbol, label), so meaning holds even without color.
- Use semantic colors sparingly so their meaning stays recognizable.
- Do not use danger red, warning yellow, success green, or info blue as general-purpose accent colors.
