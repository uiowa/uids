# Color

Our black and gold color palette is distinct across higher education and serves as the foundation for Iowa brand recognition. Our system builds on that foundation to define how color consistently works across the formats and mediums we use to reach our audiences.

## Use color intentionally

Each of the color palettes developed within our system serve a specific function and purpose:

- **Primary colors** reinforce the brand identity and create an immediate connection to Iowa.
- **Neutral colors** support legibility, structure, and hierarchy across text, backgrounds, borders, and dividers.
- **Accent colors** add emphasis and create visual distinction for small, intentional details.
- **Status colors** have universally recognized meanings related to status, risk, safety, or required action.
- **Data visualization colors** make charts and graphs clear, readable, and easy to compare.

Every color on this page is read from the design tokens in `src/tokens`.

## Primary colors

Black and gold are Iowa's primary colors and should be the most prominent colors used to clearly connect your program and message to Iowa. Use the correct color values for the medium you're working in to achieve the most accurate gold.

<Story of={ColorStories.Primary} />

### Guidance

- Black and gold should be the most prominent colors in every communication.
- Use Iowa gold at full strength. Tinting, darkening, or using lighter opacities loses recognition.
- Never use gold text on white, or white text on gold. That pairing fails accessibility contrast. Use black text instead.
- Reserve gold text for short headlines and callouts on dark backgrounds. Never use it for body copy. Use white for body text on black instead.

## Neutral colors

Neutral colors can help create structure, improve legibility, and organize information as text, backgrounds, borders, and dividers. They help establish hierarchy and clear space alongside Iowa’s primary colors.

<Story of={ColorStories.Neutral} />

Each neutral is a token named `--uiowa-color-neutral-{step}`, for example `--uiowa-color-neutral-100`.

### When to use

- Text and type hierarchy
- Page, section, and component backgrounds
- Borders, dividers, table rows, and interface structure
- Disabled, inactive, or secondary controls
- Light and dark mode foundations
- Presentation backgrounds, gridlines, and chart structure

### Guidance

- Use dark neutrals for text displayed on light backgrounds.
- Use light neutrals for backgrounds, rows, and dividers. Use darker neutrals to create structure.
- Use light neutrals for text displayed on dark backgrounds.
- Don't place gold over light neutral backgrounds. Gold does not have enough contrast and does not meet accessibility standards.

## Accent colors

Accent colors add emphasis, create visual distinction, and draw attention to key details. Use them on small, intentional elements rather than across large areas or as the primary color of a layout.

Accent colors work best when used sparingly. If a layout relies on repeated accents to make important information stand out, strengthen the hierarchy, spacing, or copy before adding more color.

<Story of={ColorStories.Accent} />

### When to use

- Calls to action and links within approved styles
- Key details that need added visibility or distinction
- Font Awesome icons and interface icons
- Badges, tags, and other small, contained components
- Fills and supporting graphic elements used in limited areas
- Functional states or interactions defined by a component

### Guidance

- Keep accent colors to no more than 10% of a layout. Use primary colors and neutrals for larger areas so black and gold remain prominent.
- Do not place accent colors directly against Iowa gold. The combination can make both colors harder to distinguish and reduce brand recognition.
- Do not use accent colors for headlines, labels, body copy, or other text. Links may use the approved blue style but should always be underlined.
- Check contrast before placing text on an accent-colored background. Use black text only on the accent colors.
- Do not use an accent color as the primary or identifying color for a college, school, center, institute, department, unit, or campaign. Keep these identities anchored in black and gold.

## Data visualization colors

Data visualization colors distinguish categories, groups, or series in charts, graphs, and other data-driven statistics. The palette supports clear, accessible comparisons, but color should never carry meaning on its own. Pair data colors with labels, patterns, position, or other visual cues.

### When to use

- Charts and graphs with multiple categories, groups, or series
- Data points such as bars, lines, points, areas, or segments
- Legends and keys that identify those categories or series
- Comparisons that need clear visual separation across a visualization

<Story of={ColorStories.DataVisualization} />

Each data color is a token named `--uiowa-color-data-visualization-categorical-{number}`, numbered 1 to 12.

### Guidance

- Use data colors in the order they appear. The sequence is designed to keep adjacent colors easy to distinguish. Do not reorder the palette based only on visual preference.
- Use the data palette only to distinguish categories, groups, or series within charts and visualizations. For emphasis elsewhere in a layout, use the accent palette.
- Do not rely on color alone to explain the data. Pair data colors with labels, legends, patterns, position, or other visual cues so categories and series remain clear.

## Status colors

Status colors communicate widely recognized meanings such as state, risk, safety, information, or required action. They apply familiar associations from safety, signage, wayfinding, status indicators, and everyday use.

### When to use

- Alerts and messages
- Validation, confirmation, and error states
- System feedback and status indicators
- Safety, caution, stop, or danger cues
- Informational notices and wayfinding cues
- Environmental or operational contexts where color identifies a role, risk, or required action

### Values

<Story of={ColorStories.Status} />

Each status has an edge token, `--uiowa-color-{status}`, and a background token, `--uiowa-color-{status}-surface`.

### Guidance

- Use status colors only when they communicate a familiar, widely recognized meaning, such as status, risk, safety, information, identification, or required action.
- Pair status colors with words, symbols, icons, patterns, shapes, or placement so people do not have to rely on color alone to understand the meaning.
- Use each status color sparingly and only for its assigned meaning so it remains recognizable across applications.
